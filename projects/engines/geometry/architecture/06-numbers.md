# 6. Numbers: the grid, exact predicates and tolerances

*Part of the [Spare Kit geometry kernel architecture](../ARCHITECTURE.md). Decided in [G01](../../../kit/notes/G01-geometry-kernel.md); section numbers match across the set.*

This section decides the foundation everything else stands on. Evidence: [05](../../../kit/notes/G01-evidence/05-exact-arithmetic-determinism.md), with [01](../../../kit/notes/G01-evidence/01-manifold-mesh-booleans.md) for plane-based booleans and [02](../../../kit/notes/G01-evidence/02-occt-parasolid-acis.md) for tolerant modelling.

## 6.1 The grid

Every coordinate the kernel stores is a whole number of grid steps.

- **The standard grid step, q0, is 156.25 nanometres: 1/6400 of a millimetre.** It is the largest step that makes both 0.01 mm (64 steps) and 1/256 inch (635 steps) exact whole numbers, so metric and imperial models both round-trip exactly. One millimetre is 6,400 steps and one inch is 162,560.
- **Coordinates are `i64` in a local frame, kept within 2^40 steps of its origin: ±171.8 km at q0.** The range is ±(2^40 − 1) steps, both ends included; 2^40 itself is refused (`geom_num::grid::COORD_MAX`, G02). That covers any building, any site and a 50 km road corridor in one frame. Keeping within 40 bits is what lets every predicate below run in fixed-size integers, whatever the step.
- **Frames hold the real world.** Each model has a project frame. Its link to a map projection (for example NZTM2000) is a frame record: the coordinate system's ID, the origin in grid steps (an NZTM northing is about 2^45 steps), a rotation and a scale factor. Survey and map data are converted into the frame once, in double-double arithmetic, and rounded to the grid, ties to even. Double-double alone cannot round correctly next to a whole step or a midpoint, so every value within 2^-40 of one is settled exactly in wide integers (section 17.1). Networks wider than ±171.8 km, such as a regional road model, use several frames on a shared lattice, so moving between neighbouring frames is an exact integer shift.
- **A ladder of grid steps, each exactly 25 times finer than the one above.** Every frame picks one:

  | Step | Size | Exact units | Range per frame | For |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | q0 | 156.25 nm | 0.01 mm, 1 mm, 1/256 inch | ±171.8 km | Buildings, sites, roads, survey |
  | q1 | 6.25 nm | 1 µm, 0.01 mm, 1 mil (1/1000 inch), 1/256 inch | ±6.87 km | Mechanical parts, products, circuit boards |
  | q2 | 0.25 nm | 1 nm, and every unit in the rows above | ±275 m | Reserved, for example chip layout |

  A finer step divides every coarser one exactly, so a building's coordinates convert into a part's fine frame by multiplying by 25, with nothing lost. A precision part or a circuit board placed in a building keeps its own fine frame, and a boolean between the two converts the building's geometry into the fine frame and stays exact. Converting fine to coarse rounds, and the result says so. Why 25: 156.25 nm is the largest step making 0.01 mm and 1/256 inch exact, and 6.25 nm, which is 156.25 nm divided by 25, is the largest step that also makes 1 µm and 1 mil exact (it is the greatest common divisor of 1 µm, 1 mil, 0.01 mm and 1/256 inch). The US survey foot (1,200/3,937 m) is a whole number of steps on none of them, except in multiples of 3,937 feet (24.11). This is decision 1 in section 32.
- **Angles have a unit of their own: proposed in section 17.2, pending decision (31.7, chapter 17).** Survey angles and site-grid rotations are stored as whole **angle units** (`AngleUnits`, an `i64`), each 1/2,500 of an arc-second. It is the largest unit that makes both 0.01″ and 0.00001 gon (0.0324″) whole numbers, as q0 does for 0.01 mm and 1/256 inch. A full circle is 3,240,000,000 units, and ε_ang (section 6.5) is about a thirtieth of one unit. Finer angles round half to even to the nearest unit. The word "step" is kept for lengths only. Until the unit is decided and built (piece "Angle units and core COGO"), the Ground frames built in G03 store their rotation as an integer direction instead (17.1).

Why a grid and not floating point: Revit and OpenCascade store doubles and pay for it with tolerances that leak into every decision. On a grid, the data the kernel decides from is exact, and the only rounding happens at known points: when a new point is committed (at most half a step per coordinate, 78 nm at q0, ties to even (6.3); as a distance in 3D, at most (√3/2)q, about 135 nm) and when curved geometry is fitted (at most the certified bound below).

## 6.2 Exact predicates

A predicate is a yes, no or "exactly on it" question: which side of this plane is this point, does this point lie inside this circle. Topology is decided only by predicates, and every predicate in the kernel is exact.

- **They return a sign, never a number:** `Negative`, `Zero` or `Positive`. In `geom-num` each predicate returns `Result<Sign, NumError>`. The error, `BudgetExceeded`, can come only from a defect in the crate's own checked arithmetic, never from an input, and it is never turned into a sign: `Zero` would claim an exact degeneracy nobody showed. For grid points the result is always `Ok` (G02, `predicate.rs`). Section 22 maps the error to the kind `Internal`.
- **Sign conventions.** `orient2d` and `incircle` have the signs of Shewchuk's `predicates.c`. `orient3d` and `insphere` have the opposite signs: `orient3d(a, b, c, d)` is positive when `d` lies on the side that the right-hand normal (b − a) × (c − a) points to, while `predicates.c` is positive when `d` lies below that plane. Code ported from Shewchuk-based libraries, and any check against them (28.6), negates those two.
- **They are computed in fixed-size integers.** With coordinates under 2^40 (B = 40 bits), orientation in 2D and 3D fits in `i128`; circle and sphere tests in 256 bits; the deepest boolean test (a point made by three planes, tested against a fourth plane) in 384 bits. That is the budget for booleans and every arrangement predicate (section 11.3). Each predicate's module carries the written derivation of its bit budget and a test at the extreme values (every coordinate at ±(2^40 − 1)). Evidence: the bit-budget table in [05](../../../kit/notes/G01-evidence/05-exact-arithmetic-determinism.md), section 4, matching EMBER's published 256-bit bound at 26-bit inputs. In `geom-num` (G02) every fixed-width step is a checked method, never a native operator, and each derivation's arithmetic is asserted when the crate compiles, so a wrong figure stops the build. Behind each budget is a total path that no grid input reaches: the explicit predicates evaluate again from the raw `i64` coordinates in `I384`, which holds every intermediate for any `i64` input, and the plane classification and commit of 6.3 repeat their whole computation in `I512`. A step that still failed returns `BudgetExceeded`. So a wrong budget costs time, never a wrong sign, in any build profile.
- **Planes have a budget too.** A plane through three grid points has integer coefficients. The plane type refuses, at construction, a normal over 2B+3 = 83 bits or an offset over 3B+5 = 125 bits at B = 40 ([05](../../../kit/notes/G01-evidence/05-exact-arithmetic-determinism.md), section 4), and the 384-bit budget assumes these limits. They are safe budgets, not the most that grid points reach. With M = 2^40 − 1, a plane through three grid points has |n_i| ≤ 4M² (82 bits) and |d| ≤ 4M³ (122 bits); a grid point's side value |n·p + d| ≤ 16M³ (124 bits); a three-plane point's weight |w| ≤ 256M^6 (248 bits) and numerators |X_i| ≤ 256M^7 (288 bits); and classifying that point against a fourth plane, |n·X + d·w| ≤ 4096M^9 (372 bits). Planes and points built from the corners of the frame's cube reach each of these exactly (G02, `plane.rs`, tested).
- **Some predicates outside booleans need wider integers.** Other chapters propose these, still fixed-size and behind the same generated filters, pending an owner's decision:

  | Predicate | Bits at B = 40 | Type | Proposed in |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | Order of two straight-skeleton (roof) events by height (two three-plane points) | 13B+27 = 547 | `I576` | 13.15; chapter 13, open question 2 |
  | Order of two hidden-line events along an edge, view along a grid axis | about 13B+27 = 547 | `I576` | 18.6; chapter 18, open question 1 |
  | The same, for any view direction and for perspective | about 15B+33 = 633 | `I640` | 18.6; chapter 18, open question 1 |
  | Order of two mitre-offset events in time (four moving planes) | about 720 | `I768` | 13.11; chapter 13, open question 2 |
  | Pencil predicates for two quadrics in general position | several thousand, by a rough count | To be derived | 12.7; chapter 12, open question 7 |
  | 2D predicates on rational constructions (rational directions, rational centres) | not yet derived; possibly several thousand (unverified) | To be derived | 14; chapter 14, open question 2 |

  Each needs what the 384-bit tests have: a written derivation of its bit budget, a test at the extreme values and a generated filter. Each also costs more. By section 23.2's rule, a schoolbook product grows with the square of its number of 64-bit limbs, so a product in `I576`, `I640` or `I768` (9, 10 or 12 limbs) costs about 5, 6 or 9 times one in 256 bits. Section 23's cost model, section 28.7's proofs and section 29.3's list of wide integers cover only `I256`, `I320` and `I384` so far; each wider type an owner accepts joins them there. Code already holds wider types that wait for that acceptance (31.7, chapter 6). `geom-num`'s `I512` (G02) is the total path above and adds no budget of its own. `geom-math` (G03) settles frame conversions and general transforms exactly in `Wide<4>` (256 bits), `Wide<10>` (640), `Wide<20>` (1,280), `Wide<34>` (2,176) and `Wide<40>` (2,560) (section 17.1). `I576`, `I640` and `I768` do not exist yet.
- **A cheap filter goes first.** Each predicate first runs in `f64` with a semi-static error bound (the method of Meyer and Pion's generator, as used in Geogram's PCK, BSD-3) and returns at once when the sign is certain. Only uncertain cases fall through to the exact integer path. There is no interval stage and no change of rounding mode, which Rust forbids and WebAssembly cannot do. A construction that rounds to the grid may put a double-double stage between the two: `geom-math`'s general transform (G03) runs `f64`, then double-double, then exact integers. The explicit-point predicates (G02, G28) run their generated filters (G27) first, except orient2d on 64-bit targets other than WebAssembly, where the filter was measured to cost more than the exact path it would save; that exception is 23.2's proposed amendment, pending an owner's decision (section 32).
- **Filter constants are generated, not hand-typed**, from a small description of each predicate, the way Geogram's PCK generator does it. Hand-typed error bounds are where predicate libraries go wrong.
- **Degenerate ties are handled on purpose.** Where an algorithm needs every sign to be non-zero (Delaunay triangulation, ray parity), it uses symbolic perturbation (Simulation of Simplicity, Edelsbrunner and Mücke 1990), ordered by persistent names, never by memory address as Geogram's default does. Where a tie means something real (two walls sharing a face), booleans handle it explicitly, because a perturbation would turn a shared face into an arbitrary tiny gap or overlap.

## 6.3 Constructions stay implicit until committed

A construction makes a new point or plane: where three planes meet, where a line hits a plane. Rounding a constructed point and then deciding things about the rounded point is the classic way kernels lose consistency (CGAL's own manual warns of it; [05](../../../kit/notes/G01-evidence/05-exact-arithmetic-determinism.md), section 3).

- Inside an operation, constructed points stay implicit: a point is stored as "the meeting of these three planes" in homogeneous integer coordinates, and predicates act on that definition exactly (the method of Attene's indirect predicates and EMBER's plane-based arithmetic, re-derived in our own code because Attene's library is LGPL and changes the rounding mode).
- **What an implicit point stores.** In `geom-num` (G02) a three-plane point stores its three planes only, and computes its homogeneous coordinates (X, w) again each time it is classified or committed: three `I256` cross products, a dot product and nine `I320` products. Storing (X, w) would cost 152 more bytes per point and remove those recomputations. Section 23's cost model decides the trade (31.7, chapter 23).
- **The implicit points built so far (G29).** `geom-num` has three, each stored as its definition and classified exactly through its generated filter first (6.2): the three-plane point (`plane::Tpi`, classified against a fourth plane by `Plane::side_of_tpi`, the 4 × 4 determinant of the planes' coefficients in `I384` times the sign of its weight); the point where the line through two grid points meets a plane (`plane::Lpi`, by `Plane::side_of_lpi`, w₁ s₂ₚ − w₂ s₁ₚ in `I256` times the sign of w₁, at most 256M^6 < 2^253); and the point where two 2D lines cross (`crossing::Ssi`, by `Ssi::orient2d`, oa · ob₂ − ob · oa₂ in `I256` times the sign of its weight, at most 32M⁴ < 2^165). All three commit to the grid through one shared rounding. Each module header gives the bit budget; `tests/attack_g29.rs` checks every sign against a big-integer oracle in parametric form, at the frame's corners and one step from degenerate.
- **Flat-faced solids stay plane-based for good.** A polyhedral solid is stored as its planes, and every vertex is a meeting of planes. Booleans make new solids out of the same input planes, so cutting a wall 300 times never rounds anything and never drifts (section 11).
- A point is rounded to the grid only when it must become explicit (export, a 2D drawing, a curved construction). The rounded result is re-checked with exact predicates. If rounding would flip anything, the implicit form is kept instead.
- **How a commit rounds.** Each coordinate goes to the nearest step, ties to even, decided exactly in wide integers, as 6.1 rounds angles. So a coordinate at exactly 2^40 − 1/2 rounds to 2^40, which is outside the frame, and the commit fails with `FrameRangeExceeded` (`Commit`). Converting a coordinate from one ladder step to a coarser one never ties, because it divides by a power of 25, which is odd. G02 chose this tie rule, following 6.1's rule for angles. It belongs to the ladder, so the owner accepts or rejects it with the ladder (32.5; 31.7, chapter 6).

## 6.4 Curved geometry: deterministic and certified

Curves and surfaces other than lines and planes are evaluated in `f64` arithmetic in grid units, which Rust computes exactly to IEEE rules on every supported target (section 21). RFC 3514 itself names the exception, 32-bit x86 without SSE2, which can round twice; it is not supported. Kernel crates are `no_std`, and `core` has no `f64::mul_add`, so kernel code writes a fused multiply-add as `geom_num::math::fma`.

The maths functions come only from the kernel's own maths module, `geom_num::math`, never the platform's, because the standard ones differ by platform (section 21). The module has two sources:

- **The proven functions:** `sinpi`, `cospi`, `exp`, `ln` and `atan2`, correctly rounded ports of CORE-MATH in the kernel's own source (G05), with interval versions of the first four. The proven angle form is half-turns: there is no proven radian `sin` or `cos`.
- **The `libm` crate:** `sqrt`, `fma`, `floor`, `ceil` and round-to-even, which IEEE 754 defines exactly, and, kept apart in `math::unproven`, the functions with no proven bound: radian `sin` and `cos`, `tan`, `asin`, `acos`, `atan`, `log2`, `log10`, `pow`, `powi`, `hypot` and `cbrt`, and `libm`'s own versions of the five proven functions. Nothing certified rests on them (12.3).

`libm` is pure Rust, but it is not the same code on every target: on aarch64 with NEON it uses the processor's square root and fused multiply-add; on x86-64 with SSE2, the processor's square root, and its fused multiply-add only where a check at run time finds the processor has one, its own routine otherwise; and on wasm32 its own routines, because its WebAssembly intrinsics are off. The bits agree because IEEE 754 rounds those operations correctly. On wasm32 the last bit of `libm`'s square root rests on `libm`'s testing, not on a proof, so `geom-num` holds `math::sqrt` bit for bit to an exact oracle, and CI runs that test on wasm32 (G02).

Every curved computation that feeds a decision carries a certified bound from interval or Bernstein arithmetic, rounded outward with `next_up` and `next_down` (stable in Rust since 1.86). Every rounded bound is widened, even an exact one, so an interval never certifies a zero: a computed zero has no certain sign, and zeros are decided by the exact predicates (6.2). Quantities that are never negative by construction use the interval square and the interval sum of squares, which know it, so even the distance between coincident points is certified (G02, `Interval::square` and `Interval::norm`).

## 6.5 The tolerance ladder

There is no per-entity tolerance that grows. There is one fixed ladder, derived from the frame's grid step q, so it scales with the step: a circuit board's fit bound is 25 times tighter than a building's.

| Name | In steps | At q0 (buildings) | At q1 (parts, boards) | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Commit rounding | at most q/2 per coordinate, ties to even (6.3); as a distance, (√2/2)q in 2D and (√3/2)q in 3D | 78 nm per axis; 110 nm in 2D, 135 nm in 3D | 3.1 nm per axis; 4.4 nm in 2D, 5.4 nm in 3D | Making an implicit point explicit |
| Fit bound, ε_fit | 64q | 0.01 mm | 0.4 µm | The largest certified error of any curved construction (intersection curves, fitted surfaces, offsets of NURBS) that feeds topology |
| Angular bound, ε_ang | 2^-34 rad | about 5.8 × 10^-11 rad | the same | Angles in curved code only. It equals ε_fit over the frame's range (64q over 2^40 q), so it is the same at every step; the same construction as Parasolid's 1e-11 |
| Import healing, ε_heal | 640q by default, capped at 6,400q | 0.1 mm, capped at 1 mm | 4 µm, capped at 40 µm | Closing gaps in imported data, once, at import, recorded in the operation log |
| Short-edge warning | 3,200q | 0.5 mm | 20 µm | A warning only. There is no hard minimum edge length; the true minimum is one grid step |

- The exact polyhedral core uses no tolerance at all.
- **In code.** `geom-num`'s ladder module (G02) holds the commit rung per coordinate as `COMMIT_PER_AXIS`, one half step, and the two distance rungs as a certified ceiling of one whole step, `COMMIT_DISTANCE_2D` and `COMMIT_DISTANCE_3D`, because a count of whole steps cannot hold √n/2 (n·(1/2)² ≤ 1 for n ≤ 4, checked when the crate compiles). Code that needs the tighter (√n/2)q uses the derivation, not the constant. The proposed rungs below, h_min among them, join the module only when an owner accepts them.
- **Tolerant bodies exist only as an import state.** An imported body whose edges do not quite meet carries per-edge tolerances up to ε_heal, marked Tolerant. No operation ever raises a tolerance. Operations either work within the existing ones or refuse with `ToleranceExceeded`, and healing turns a Tolerant body into a Certified or Exact one. This is Parasolid's published model ("exact by default, tolerant only where needed") with OpenCascade's monotone growth removed.
- Every operation has a ceiling on the certified bound it may return: ε_fit, unless the caller accepts more. Exceeding it is an error that rolls back, never silent growth (Parasolid's `max_tol`, made mandatory). That error is `ExactnessNotAccepted`; `ToleranceExceeded` is only for Tolerant data (section 22). `geom-num`'s `Exactness::within` and `combine_within` (G02) report a bound over the ceiling as `NumError::ToleranceExceeded`; the operation that wraps them reports it as `ExactnessNotAccepted` (22).

**Proposed rungs, pending decision.** Principle 10 and section 27 require every threshold to come from this ladder. Other chapters need values, or new meanings, that the table above does not hold. They are listed here with their derivations, without changing that table. An owner accepts or rejects each; an accepted rung moves into the table above and into the ladder module in `geom-num`. Chapters cite each by its name here.
