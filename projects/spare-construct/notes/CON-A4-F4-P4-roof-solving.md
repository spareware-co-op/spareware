# CON-A4-F4-P4: Roof solving (ridges, hips and valleys, first half)

**Status, 2026-11-23:** Decided for the build (piece A69, job 6.2). construct-roofs' `solve` module follows it; its [FORMAT.md](../crates/construct-roofs/FORMAT.md) is the stored form, unchanged in version 1 (a slope per edge was already in the record); construct-regen's `roof_plan` reads a solved roof's filled recipe and its `kernel` module's child `roof` builds it through the engine seam; the roof commands make hips with form `hip`. The page's Roof tool offers the form hip and a pitch per edge, and draws the lines the filled recipe carries (`lines`); the two-browser test makes a hip roof. Joins between roofs (the other half of CON-A4-F4-P4) are later.

## The question

Job 5.3 built a roof from a footprint with a slope per edge, but only flat, mono-pitch and even gables on a rectangle along the plan's axes; anything else was refused, naming job 6.2. A roof with a slope per edge should solve itself: where each pair of its planes meets (a ridge at the top, a hip at an outer corner, a valley at an inner one), on a rectangle hipped all round, a hip at one end and a verge at the other, a rectangle turned off the axes, an L or any other right-angled footprint, and a gable whose two slopes differ. Every value had to stay exact on the grid (LENGTH-UNIT.md), every layer had to be built through the engine trait, and what the method cannot hold had to be refused by name.

## How roofs are set out

A hipped roof's planes each rise from an eaves line at the roof's pitch. Where two meet at a corner of the building the line between them (the hip) runs from the corner up to the ridge; seen from above it halves the corner when the two pitches are one. At an inside corner the two planes meet in a valley, falling to the corner. A ridge is where two opposite planes meet, level. A gable end is a plane that does not slope: the slopes beside it run on to it and stop. Carpenters set these out from the plan alone when every pitch is one: each hip and valley at half the corner, the ridge midway between opposite eaves.

## The decisions

- **The method is the straight skeleton** (Aichholzer, Aurenhammer, Alberts and Gaertner, 1995): with every sloped edge at one pitch, a roof's planes meet where the eaves outline's edges, moved inward at one speed, meet. An edge that does not slope is an edge that does not move, as in the weighted straight skeleton (Eppstein and Erickson, 1999) with weight zero. Two cases are solved, each exactly:
  - a **convex** footprint (a rectangle, along the axes or turned): the roof is the lower envelope of its sloped planes, so each plane's face is the outline clipped by the half-planes where it is the lowest;
  - a **right-angled footprint with an inside corner**, every edge sloping: the straight skeleton of a right-angled polygon is its L-infinity medial axis, so the roof at depth `t` is the outline eroded by a square of half-side `t`, which is the union of the outline's maximal rectangles each shrunk by `t`. That union changes only where a low side of one rectangle meets a high side of another; between two such depths every moving edge sweeps a trapezoid of its plane. The roof is those trapezoids, each flat by construction (its two parallel sides lie at two depths).
- **It is ours.** The method is a few hundred lines of plane geometry in exact fractions (num-rational, MIT OR Apache-2.0); the skeleton and polygon crates we may use work in floats, which cannot say whether two planes meet exactly and differ in the last place between builds. Its header names the method.
- **Footprints.** Right-angled: every corner 90 or 270 degrees, so every edge runs along one direction `u` or square to it. In the frame `U = p x + q y`, `V = -q x + p y` (`(p, q)` the first edge's smallest whole step) the footprint lies along the axes and every meeting point is a fraction. Along the plan's axes the frame is the plan.
- **Pitches.** A solved roof's sloped edges rise at one pitch. Two pitches are refused where their planes meet (`UnevenPitches`), because a layer's thickness square to each slope stands its faces at a different height above each plane, so the line where two faces meet moves from face to face to points no single rounding keeps on the grid in a flat face. The one exception is the **uneven gable on a rectangle along the axes**: its two slopes meet in one ridge, each face's ridge found exactly where its two slopes meet (`(s' W + u' - u) / (s + s')` across), and each layer is still its section lofted from verge to verge, so it is built exactly. At one pitch that rule gives the even gable's ridge, midway, bit for bit as version 1 built it.
- **Overhangs.** On a rectangle along the axes, any overhang per edge (the lower envelope places a hip that meets a deeper eaves where it does). On a footprint with an inside corner, one overhang all round (`OverhangsDiffer` otherwise), the eaves outline each edge moved out square to itself, and the roof the skeleton of that outline. On a footprint off the axes, none (`OverhangOffAxis`): an edge moved out square to itself there has no corner on the grid.
- **Verges.** On a convex footprint an edge with no pitch is a verge: the slopes beside it run on to it. On a footprint with an inside corner every edge slopes (`VergeOnReflex`): gable ends there are not solved yet.
- **Onto the grid, and where a value is not exact.** Every meeting point is worked as an exact fraction, then rounded once, by one rule: along the axes to the nearest grid point (a half up), turned to the nearest point on even steps (so every plane's plan area is a whole number of square steps), each height to the nearest step (turned, by whole-number square roots of `s h / sqrt(p^2 + q^2)`). A point met by several planes is one value and rounds once for all of them. Along the axes, on a footprint whose corners are on even steps (every corner on the hundredth of a millimetre is), no meeting point needs rounding in plan, so only heights are rounded, each by under half a step (0.08 micrometres); a hip's or valley's ends are then exact in plan. A turned footprint's meeting points may move by under one step in plan; a face whose rounded corners are not on one plane is cut into triangles from its first corner, each flat, so the face is creased by less than a step (far under the model's 0.01 mm). Plan areas, volumes and the sums are exact from those corners; a slope area is the nearest square step to the plan area times `sqrt(1 + s^2)`, plane by plane, and a solved roof's (or an uneven gable's) whole slope area is its planes' sum, so the planes always add up to the whole.
- **Each layer is one solid, through the engine trait alone.** At one pitch every face of a layer stands one height above the bottom face on every plane, so each layer is the bottom face's flat pieces, each moved up to the layer's lower face and taken straight up to its upper face, asked of the engine as a loft from the piece to the same piece moved up (`Engine::loft`, whose walls are flat faces through grid corners; solidcore's extrusion of a sloped profile makes a spline surface the kernel refuses), united in an order where each piece shares a side with one before it (`Engine::unite`, one of the flat-faced booleans of decision 32.70), so the union grows as one solid. Its volume is its plan area times its upright height, exactly. No method was added to the engine trait. Every result is the engine's, so the kernel's checker has passed it.
- **Lines and planes are data.** Each plane of the top face is the edge it rises from, its flat pieces, its plan area and its slope area; each line is a `ridge`, `hip`, `valley`, `eave` or `verge` with its two ends, joined where it runs straight on. A crease between two planes is a ridge where the planes face each other and the fold is convex, a hip where it is convex otherwise, a valley where it is concave (decided from the two planes' normals, exactly). They are worked out each time and never stored, for the page and the drawings to come; the filled recipe the builders are handed carries the lines (`lines`, read by no builder) so the page draws them as the server gave them and works out none itself.
- **The ridge height** of a solved roof rising from two edges or more is its top face's highest point; with one edge (a mono-pitch roof on a turned footprint) it has none, as a mono-pitch roof has none.
- **The commands carry it** without a new command: a slope per edge was already in the record. `roof.create` and `roof.form` take form `hip`, rising from the edges `eaves` names, or every edge; `roof.pitch` with `edge` on a gable makes an uneven gable. The stored form is construct-roofs' own (`solved`, or `mono` or `gable` where the edges make one).
- **What is refused**, by name: a footprint not right-angled (`NotRightAngled`), two pitches where planes meet, a verge or two overhangs on a footprint with an inside corner, an overhang off the axes, an arc edge (construct-slabs refuses it), a hole (openings in roofs are job 4.2's), and anything the rounding would fold (`NotExact`) or the method cannot cut into pieces that meet side to side (`NotSolved`). Nothing here is interim; the engine behind the seam is, as its register row says.

## Why

- A slope per edge is how a roof is drawn and specified; the planes, their lines and their areas follow from it, so they are worked out and never stored.
- Exact fractions first and one rounding per point keep every plane meeting its neighbours bit for bit and every face flat, as the wall's and the simple roof's rules do.
- Pieces taken straight up and united use only what the engine already makes exactly (flat-faced lofts and their union), so no engine method was needed.

## Not decided here

- Unequal pitches at hips and valleys; gable ends and two overhangs on footprints with inside corners; footprints with edges at other angles; arcs.
- Joins between roofs, dormers (CON-A4-F3-P4), openings (job 4.2), soffits, fascias and gutters.
