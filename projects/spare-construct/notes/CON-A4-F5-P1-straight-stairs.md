# CON-A4-F5-P1: Straight stairs laid out by rules (first half)

**Status, 2026-11-28:** Decided for the build (piece A82, the data, shape and commands half of job 6.7; CON-A4-F5-P1 in part and CON-A4-F5-P2). The crate `construct-stairs` follows it and its [FORMAT.md](../crates/construct-stairs/FORMAT.md) is the stored form; construct-regen's `stairs` lays a stair out from its levels, its `stair_plan` reads the filled recipe for both builders, and its `kernel` module's child `stair` builds it through the engine seam; construct-api's `cloud::stairs` holds the commands and the server's view. The page's stair tool, drawing a stair in plan and 3D, and its section of the two-browser test are the next lane's.

## The question

A stair is the building element an architect checks most against the code: its risers, goings and pitch, its landings and its headroom are set by the building code of the country it is built in, and a stair that does not meet them is redrawn. Job 6.7 asks for a straight stair with landings laid out by rules, the code's limits held as data (New Zealand's clause D1 first). The comparison with an architect's sample model (27 stairs, 131 railings, 2 ramps in one building) showed stairs as a large gap, and railings (job 14.2) and ramps (job 14.3) are to build on this crate. Before commands and pages could make stairs, this half had to settle what a stair holds, which limits it is laid out by and where they come from, how a floor-to-floor height that does not divide evenly is laid out exactly, what happens when no layout meets the limits, and where every corner of its solids lies on the kernel's grid.

## How stairs are set out

A flight rises from one floor or landing to the next in risers of one height on treads of one going (the horizontal distance from one nosing to the next); its pitch is the angle of the line through the nosings. A designer takes the floor-to-floor height, divides it by the highest riser the code allows, takes the next whole number of risers, and so has the actual riser; a flight of `n` risers has `n - 1` treads, its top riser rising onto the landing or the floor above, so its length in plan is `(n - 1)` goings. A landing breaks a flight that would rise too far, and a quarter or half landing turns the stair (an L or a U). A concrete stair's structure is a waist under the steps, its least thickness measured square to its underside from the steps' inner corners.

## The code: D1/AS1

New Zealand's Building Code clause D1 (Access routes) is met for stairs by its Acceptable Solution D1/AS1 ([D1/AS1, second edition, amendment 6, effective 1 January 2017](https://www.building.govt.nz/assets/Uploads/building-code-compliance/d-access/d1-access-routes/asvm/d1-access-routes-2nd-edition-amendment6.pdf), read in full for its section 1 and section 4; its Figures 2, 3, 11 and 12 read from the drawings themselves). What it sets for a stair:

- **Table 6 (paragraph 4.1.1):** the most pitch, most riser and least tread for each category of stairway: service and minor private 47 degrees, 220 mm, 220 mm; secondary private 41 degrees, 200 mm, 250 mm; common and main private 37 degrees, 190 mm, 280 mm; accessible 32 degrees, 180 mm, 310 mm. The categories are defined in its Definitions.
- **Figure 11 (paragraph 4.1.1):** the riser, tread and pitch line slope charted together: pitch line slopes from 23 to 47 degrees, risers from 150 to 220 mm, tread depths from 220 to 400 mm; the pitch is the angle whose tangent is the riser over the going, the going being the tread depth less any nosing projection (Figure 12). Paragraph 1.2.1 and Figure 2 give stairs from 23 degrees up, and paragraph 4.1.1's Comment 2 says a pitch under 23 degrees does not allow an acceptable gait.
- **Paragraph 4.1.3:** riser height and tread depth uniform within 5 mm in one flight.
- **Paragraph 4.2.1:** at least 900 mm between handrails on an accessible stairway, or between handrail and wall on a common one; no least width for a stairway in a household unit.
- **Paragraphs 4.3.1 to 4.3.4 and Table 7:** landings at the top and bottom of every flight; at most 2.5 m rise between landings for common and accessible stairways and 4.0 m for private and service ones; a landing at least as wide as the route it serves and at least 900 mm long.
- **Paragraph 1.4.1, Table 1 and Figure 3:** 2000 mm clear height above the pitch line of a stairway and over a landing less than 2 m long.

BRANZ's bulletin on stair design ([BU625, August 2018](https://www.branz.co.nz/documents/4044/BU625-Stair-Design.pdf)) sets out the same figures from D1/AS1 and warns that the old rule of thumb of two risers and a going summing to 580 to 610 mm is not to be used in New Zealand: D1/AS1's relation between riser and going is the pitch, with the riser and going limits, and nothing else. Its worked example (2500 mm in 14 risers of 178.6 mm, 13 treads) is the layout this crate gives. The Acceptable Solution is published by the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment for everyone to use to show compliance; its figures are facts held as data with their clauses, and none of its text, tables or drawings is copied.

## The decisions

- **The limits are data, a rule set per code.** A rule set is a JSON text beside the crate (`rules/nz-d1-as1.json`), read strictly, each limit with the clause it comes from: for every stair the least pitch (23 degrees), the least riser (150 mm, the lowest Figure 11 charts), the most going (400 mm, the deepest Figure 11 charts), how far apart a flight's risers may be (5 mm), the least landing (900 mm) and the least headroom (2000 mm); per category the most pitch, the most riser, the least going, the most rise between landings and the least width where there is one. Main private and common stairways share Table 6's row and differ in Table 7 and paragraph 4.2.1, so they are two categories. Another country is another file; no code of the layout changes. Figure 11's charted riser and going ranges are held as limits because the figure gives no acceptable stair outside them.
- **A pitch is compared as a slope.** A pitch limit in whole degrees is worked as the slope a roof's pitch of the same angle is (construct-roofs' `Pitch::slope`: rise `round(10^9 tan a)` over 10^9, the simple-roof note's rule, its one float, within 5 x 10^-10 of the tangent), reused rather than written twice; a riser on a going is within it when the two compare so, crosswise, exactly. With Table 6's own least goings the pitch never binds (every category's most riser at its least going is under its most pitch), but a going set longer, a least pitch on a long going, or another code's data can make it bind, and it is checked.
- **No nosing projection yet.** The going and the tread depth are one length, as Figure 11 reads with no projection; a nosing is a later version of the recipe.
- **A stair is straight runs with landings, along the plan's axes.** It starts at the foot of its first riser on its right-hand side going up and runs along one of the plan's axes; it lies to the left of that line. A landing goes on straight, turns a quarter left or right (the next run leaves its side at its far end, so a quarter landing is at least as long as the stair is wide), or turns a half back on the left or the right (the next run comes back beside the last, the landing as wide as both, no well between). Runs along the axes keep every corner the start moved by whole lengths, on the grid with nothing rounded. A stair at another angle, winders, curved and spiral stairs are later.
- **The layout is worked exactly, never rounded to break a limit.** From the rise (the height from its base tie to its top tie, read from its levels when it is built, so a moved level lays it out again and the record never changes) the going is the stair's or its category's least; the risers the limits allow on it are `lowest = max(least riser, ceil(going x p'/q'))` and `highest = min(most riser, floor(going x p/q))`, in grid steps, for the least and most pitch's slopes; the count of risers is the least that gives risers within them, that the runs allow (two to a run at least) and as uniform as the rule set asks. A rise `H` in `n` risers is risers `floor(k H / n) - floor((k - 1) H / n)`: a height that does not divide evenly gives risers one grid step (156.25 nm) apart, far within D1/AS1's 5 mm, and their sum is the rise exactly; a rule set that asks for risers exactly alike gets a count that divides the rise or a refusal. Runs that give no count share what the others leave, as evenly as can be, the earlier taking one more.
- **When no count works, the refusal names the limits and the nearest heights that work.** The refusal gives the lowest and highest riser with the clause each comes from, the count the runs allow, and the tallest floor-to-floor height below and the lowest above that the same stair would lay out (a common stairway rising 400 mm: 380 mm or 450 mm). A run that rises past Table 7 is refused naming the clause, to add a landing.
- **The solids.** Each run is one solid: its side profile (its first riser from its base up, each tread and the riser above it, to its last tread's inner corner; then its underside back down to its base) at its right-hand side, lofted to the same profile at its left (the engine's loft of a section to the same section moved, every wall of which is flat, as a sloped roof's layer is built). Its underside is the line of its inner corners lowered square to itself by the waist: `round(waist x sqrt(L^2 + h^2) / L)` below it measured upright, by an exact integer square root, for the run's length `L` and its rise `h` from first to last nosing; it meets the run's base at a point rounded once to the grid, and the run is a solid block where the waist reaches below its base. The first run stands on the floor; a run after a landing stands on the landing's underside, so the two meet flush. Under a landing the run reaches on until its underside meets the landing's underside (that point rounded once, never past the landing's far edge), so the run and the landing meet as one piece of concrete and the run's part of its top riser is closed; the last run ends square at its last inner corner, its top riser being the floor above's edge. Each landing is its outline extruded straight up by the waist from its underside to its top. The pieces touch and are not united. Each corner is rounded at most once, by the rule its place names, and a waist too thin for the underside to pass below every step is refused rather than drawn folded.
- **Headroom inside the stair is checked exactly.** Where one piece passes over another that is not next to it, the clear height from the lower's pitch line (or its landing's top) to the upper's underside is at least the rule set's headroom everywhere over the two: worked at the corners of where they overlap, split where the upper's underside meets its base, in exact fractions. Headroom to other elements above (a floor's edge, a beam) is checked when stairs meet the floors they pass through.
- **What a stair reads back** is worked out from its layout each time and never stored, as read-only parameters a schedule lists and a tag reads: `stair.rise`, `stair.risers`, `stair.riser-height` (the tallest), `stair.treads`, `stair.going`, `stair.run-length` (the walking line in plan), `stair.pitch` (a slope) and `stair.width`.
- **Names** follow the pieces, so a railing (job 14.2) or a floor's edge can name what it meets: `run.<k>.tread.<i>` and `run.<k>.riser.<i>` for each tread, `run.<k>.side.left`, `.side.right`, `.underside` and `.end`; `landing.<k>.top`, `.underside` and `.edge.<e>`.
- **The stand-in** builds the box around the pieces and draws each as a part: a landing as its prism, a run as its profile cut upright at every corner into quadrilaterals, each a prism across the run, every point the nearest millimetre, so a page shows the steps on either edition. The material is construct-materials' default concrete until stair types carry one.
- **The commands** are `stair.create`, `stair.width`, `stair.direction` (its direction, its start, or both), `stair.runs` (its runs and landings, and its going) and `stair.delete`, each one entry and one undo step; each checks the stair the way the builders read it, so what construct-stairs refuses is refused in its words, and the generic commands are refused behind them as a room's are. Its base level is changed by `level.tie` as any element's; its top level is the one it is made with.

## Why

- Holding the limits as data, each with its clause, keeps the code's figures where a reviewer from that country can check them line by line, and makes another country a file, not a change of code (EXTENDING.md, country packs).
- Laying out by the count of risers, with risers a grid step apart where the height does not divide, is how a designer sets a stair out, and the only exact way: a riser rounded to a tidy figure would make the stair miss its floor or break a limit.
- Naming the nearest heights that work tells the person what to change (a level, a finish, a landing) instead of only that something failed.
- Lofting a run's profile across its width gives a flat-faced solid through grid corners that the engine builds today, the way the sloped roof's layers already are.

- Nosing projections, stringers and other supports, winders, curved and spiral stairs, stairs at angles off the axes, and stair types holding the category, the waist and the material.
- Changing a stair's top level and its category by command; a stair's headroom to other elements and its opening in the floor above.
- Railings (job 14.2, CON-A4-F5-P3) hosted on a stair's sides, and ramps (job 14.3, CON-A4-F5-P4) laid out by slope limits in this crate.
- Paragraph 4.3.6 (the line of sight between landings more than 8 m apart) and the handrail, lighting, slip resistance and open riser rules, which are not the layout's.
